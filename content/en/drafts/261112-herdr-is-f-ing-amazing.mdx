## The thing I wanted

I want several agents running at once. One carries the main task, another checks its work, a third digs into whatever detail the first two trip over. They hand work to each other, and I watch. That last part is where the tidy orchestration advice falls apart, because the advice is always to start the agent in the background, wait for the result, and inspect the output afterwards. I cannot work that way. A sub-agent that disappears into a process gives me nothing to look at while the expensive part happens, and the expensive part is the middle.

Sometimes an agent goes wrong and the answer still arrives neat and confident, with the mistake three paragraphs up in its reasoning where nothing in the final message hinted at it. Reading the output afterwards means reading a conclusion I have no way to audit. Most harnesses ship sub-agents of their own now, and you can switch the view to one mid-turn, but the switching costs more than it looks. Between the lead and its worker and back again, I lose the thread of both, and the window still belongs to that one harness.

I run more than one harness by choice. Every provider gives you one account, even the expensive tier runs out, and I am not going to buy the top tier of five companies to work around it. That is the appeal of [T3Code](https://github.com/pingdotgg/t3code "mention | App that runs several coding agent harnesses in one window"): several harnesses, one app, no second window. As far as I can tell it has no plugin or mod layer, so my setup would have to live inside someone else's decisions about how a workflow should look, and I would rather keep my own.

The other fix people reach for is putting every terminal on screen, which works right up until it does not. Four agents fill a screen. The fifth one costs you a quarter of the window, the ones you are not reading cost you the rest, and the whole arrangement dies with the window it lives in. I wanted somewhere the agents could live, not a wall of terminals I have to keep in sight.

## tmux did its job

tmux is a terminal that splits into panes, and for a long time that was the whole of what I asked of it. Ten agents meant ten panes, or fewer panes and a few sessions I switched back to while hunting for whichever one had gone quiet. I wrote a grid that re-tiled itself whenever a pane appeared or died, and taught it to prefer columns when the count got awkward. It worked. I want that on the record, because everything below is about a job tmux never claimed to do.

The pane is a process, and that is the entire limit. It cannot tell me whether the thing inside is thinking, waiting for me to approve something, or dead ten minutes ago while I read its scrollback for the fourth time. It cannot hand a message to another pane either, which is why my scripts kept growing a shell shim that typed into panes on my behalf. By the end I was not arranging shells anymore. I was building the thing I am about to describe, out of tmux, badly.

## Herdr TUI, found through someone else's rig

I first ran [`herdr`](https://github.com/herdrdev/herdr "mention | Agent-aware terminal workspace manager I tried before moving to core") on 21 October, and not because I had gone looking for a better multiplexer. [OpenRig](https://github.com/mvschwarz/openrig "mention | Rig project I was wiring at the time; it needed somewhere to put agent terminals") was the project I cared about that month - pods, agents with roles, a daemon keeping them alive - and its tmux runtime pushed prompts into panes through a shell shim. Six minutes after my first `herdr` command, that same rig was running with `--provider herdr` instead. I had filed herdr as a tmux alternative and never opened it once.

Inside the TUI the picture changed. Agents had names and state badges, a worker could prompt a sibling, and a wait could block on a teammate instead of on a timer I had guessed at. I put a team in one workspace and wrote the three commands that made coordination possible: `team say`, `team wait`, `team read`.

What did not hold was the layer above it. I wrote an extension bridge so an omp seat could show omp's own TUI inside the rig, opened it as [a draft PR](https://github.com/mvschwarz/openrig/pull/1747 "further-reading | The draft PR for that bridge; still open, still a draft"), and then parked it, because a better harness contract was on its way and my patch was standing in the road. That was the end of my OpenRig enthusiasm. OpenRig is what got me to open herdr. It is not what kept me.

What also did not change was the window. The team lived in that session, so seeing the team meant keeping the session open, and none of those agents ever reached the desktop app that came later. I had a place to put the herd and still had to carry it around.

## Core under the app

The thing that pulled me over was the desktop app, which is the wrong way round and also true. A post about the new client showed vertical tabs, the exact reason I had been running cmux, another terminal on the same Ghostty engine, and a few screenshots had agent status sitting next to terminal names. That was the picture I wanted and had not been able to draw. Digging into how it drew it, I found [`herdr-core`](https://github.com/herdrdev/herdr-core "mention | Headless server that runs named persistent terminals for the desktop and phone clients") underneath, which turned out to matter far more than the window I came in through.

Core runs terminals and draws nothing at all. I start one, address it by name from the `hc` CLI, give it work, wait, read the result, and the program runs without taking a pixel of my screen. The status is not a guess from a screenshot either. A program reports its own state through OSC 7501, the Program Status Protocol, and in a shell it looks like this:

```bash
printf '\e]7501;state=blocked:app=my-agent\e\\'
```

Core does not scrape terminal text and does not keep a list of agent brands. It believes the program. If the program says nothing, there is no status, and `hc wait` can do no better than wait for the process to die. That boundary is the part I care about. A badge that comes from a report means something; a badge inferred from one frame of output is decoration. Four states cover it - working, done, idle, and blocked when a human is needed - and I have not missed a fifth. I do not need to know how far along a task is. I need to know which pane is waiting on me, which one finished, and which one is still going, and those are different questions.

omp reports on its own. Hermes did not, so I wrote a small plugin, and it has worked since the first try. By the time I decided to publish it in case someone else was stuck on the same gap, someone had shipped an equivalent two days earlier. That is the entire cost of being late: fifteen minutes of work instead of an afternoon of integration, because the protocol is three lines long.

Now the team has a shape. A space for the project, a tab for the task, panes for the agents, and the lead's sub-agents folded underneath it. The folding is not decoration. I can see which worker belongs to which lead without keeping a second list in my head, and my first real core team - an omp pane that answered `READY`, a `hc wait` that returned `done` at turn 1, a pane I then deleted - was boring in exactly the way I wanted from plumbing.

## The desktop is where I work

[`herdr-app`](https://github.com/herdrdev/herdr-app "mention | Desktop client that arranges core terminals into spaces, tabs, and panes") takes those running terminals and arranges them into a workspace I sit in: projects down the side, tasks in tabs, agents in panes, sub-agents one click under the lead that started them instead of each holding a slice of the screen.

The verb I did not expect to use as much as I do is peeking. `terminal.peek` gives me a look at a terminal without moving anything; `terminal.show` gives it a pane when I decide it has earned one. For years I had assumed visibility meant a pane per worker, permanently laid out, and it turns out visibility is cheaper than that. The terminals keep working whether or not I am looking, so the only question left is which one I want to see this minute, and the answer changes constantly.

Rough edges, because there are some. `layout.apply` is destructive - it can kill and recreate shells - so I do not treat it as a harmless way to resize a grid. There is no `pane.rename` and no documented one-shot tiling verb, which is why `hgrid.py` still exists. I have not found a documented way to move a live terminal between tabs either. I would rather keep my own scripts than pretend those gaps are covered.

Core does not bring a lost agent back. After a restart of the server or the machine, a kept terminal returns as a shell in its old directory and the work that was in it is gone; resuming is not restoring. App mods are the other boundary worth saying out loud: trusted JavaScript with network access and no restriction on what it runs. I would not install one from a link I found in a thread.

## The same herd on my phone

Away from the desk, the phone is where this stops being a nice window and starts being the point. The [`Herdr` phone app](https://herdr.dev/mobile/ "reference | Official mobile client connects to herdr-core over SSH") connects to core over SSH, with no Herdr account and no vendor relay in the middle, and shows the same agents in an Inbox sorted into `needs you`, `done`, `working`, and `idle`. From there I open the live terminal, read it, type into it, and the desktop panes do not move.

April is when I last tried to solve this, and I solved it on paper: Blink, Moshi, Mosh, tmux, the whole phone-to-host chain, with a Moshi + Mosh + tmux stack that looked like it would work. I prepared the host, then never set the push token, so the notifications that were the entire point never fired once. What I wanted was to see and act on the same work from the sofa, not squint at a session that might have quietly dropped while I was away from it.

Now I open the phone and the herd is there. The app is six days old as I write this, so I will not claim the setup is frictionless for everyone. It needs `herdr-core` running, one install command, and SSH reachability, which for me means Tailscale, which is how I reach this box from anywhere anyway. In the time I have used it I have not hit a real bug. It does not offer a separate approval card and it will not let me edit the desktop layout, and when an agent needs me I open its terminal and answer there, which is what I was going to do anyway.

The TUI has its own phone story - any SSH client will do - but that is not this app. TUI sessions are separate and never appear in the Inbox at all. Getting the team onto core is what made the phone possible, and that is the part I did not appreciate when I started.

## New on top of old things

Everything underneath is older than the app itself.

The terminal is Ghostty's. `herdr-app` builds a local `ghostty-sys` crate whose manifest reads "Generated bindings to upstream libghostty-vt (vendor/ghostty)", the TUI carries `ghostty-vt`, described in-tree as "Herdr's binding to its vendored libghostty-vt", and core links the same crate. I have run Ghostty as my terminal for a long time, then through cmux, and now through this: same engine, third wrapper. That matters more than it sounds like it should. I did not have to trust a terminal in order to try this, because I had already been trusting that one for everything else.

The agents are ordinary programs. Core owns a pseudo-terminal for each terminal and starts children through `spawn_pty`; `hc run` takes any command, `hc send` types into it, `hc read` hands back the screen and the scrollback. Nothing about omp or Hermes changed in order to run here. My team script is a few hundred lines calling the same CLI any shell could call, which is why I can read it when it breaks instead of working out somebody's plugin API at midnight.

The window is not theirs either: `herdr-app` vendors GPUI, the framework from Zed, with its Apache license sitting in the tree next to the vendored Ghostty. I do not know much about what happens inside that machinery and I do not need to. What I notice is the shape, a sidebar and tabs and panes, which is the TUI in a window - something I already knew how to use on the day I installed it.

I expected to give something up and found I did not. An agent has to run inside a terminal to be seen at all, which is what the thing is. A program that does not report needs a few lines of integration, and both harnesses I run took an afternoon. A restart returns a shell in the last directory, exactly what tmux gave me before, so nothing got worse. I have not found a single thing I used to do that I cannot do here.

## It is a place, not a vendor bet

The protocol is the part I care about beyond one app. OSC 7501 is open, so a program that reports its state shows up in the same list no matter which company built it: omp reports natively, Hermes reports through a plugin I wrote, and the app needs no special relationship with either vendor to know whether its process is working or blocked. That is the difference between a place where agents live and a launcher for one company's product.

Which is why Herdr stuck. tmux still gives me panes and persistence, and I am not going to pretend it stopped working the week I changed my mind. The TUI made agents visible but kept them in a window. Core gave the terminals names and status without needing a window at all, and the desktop app and the phone let me look in whenever I have a reason to. Several agents work together, I can see where they are, and none of that depends on me holding a window open all day.

I stopped keeping every agent on screen. Now I check the herd when I need it.
