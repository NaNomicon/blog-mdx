I've been using [`Herdr`](https://herdr.dev "reference | The project site: an agent-aware multiplexer, a headless core, and desktop plus phone clients"), which is three things wearing one name: an agent-aware terminal multiplexer, a headless server that runs named terminals, and a desktop and phone client that look into that server. It is the first setup where I can run four agents at once, see which one is stuck, and answer it from the sofa - without handing any of it to a new agent runtime.

I like running a small crew. One carries the main task, another checks its work, and a third digs into whatever detail the first two trip over. They hand work to each other, and I am there.

The pattern I keep running into is the same one: spawn the sub-agent in the background, let it finish, read its summary afterwards. I cannot work that way. A background process hides the middle of the turn, and the middle is where the expensive mistakes happen.

Sometimes an agent goes off the rails early, then still hands back a tidy, confident answer - with the wrong turn buried three paragraphs up in its reasoning. Reading the output afterward means auditing a conclusion with no visibility into how it got there. Most harnesses ship sub-agents of their own now, and watching one means switching views inside that harness. Bounce between a lead and its worker a few times and you lose the thread of both, all while trapped inside one window.

I run multiple harnesses by choice. Providers give you one account, even the top tier hits rate limits (others' words, not mine), and I am not paying five companies' maximum plans just to keep moving. That is the appeal of [`T3Code`](https://github.com/pingdotgg/t3code "mention | App that runs several coding agent harnesses in one window"): several harnesses, one window. As far as I can tell, though, it has no plugin or mod layer. Adopting it means rebuilding my workflow inside someone else's opinions, and I would rather keep my own.

The other fix I reached for is putting every terminal on screen, which works right up until it does not. Four agents fill a screen. The fifth one costs you a quarter of the window, the ones you are not reading cost you the rest, and the arrangement is only ever as good as the screen you are willing to give it. I wanted somewhere the agents could live, not a wall of terminals I have to keep in sight.

## `tmux` did its job

For a long time, [`tmux`](https://github.com/tmux/tmux "reference | Terminal multiplexer; my workspace for years before this") was all I asked for. Ten agents meant ten panes, or fewer panes tucked into sessions I kept cycling through to hunt down whichever one had gone quiet. I even wrote an auto-tiling grid that rearranged itself as panes appeared and exited, preferring columns whenever the count got awkward. It worked - and I want that on the record, because everything below is about a job `tmux` never claimed to do.

To `tmux`, a pane is just a process. It cannot tell me whether the agent inside is thinking, waiting on my approval, or died ten minutes ago while I stared at its scrollback. It can type into a sibling pane with `send-keys`, but typing is not messaging, which is why my scripts kept accumulating shell shims to do it on my behalf. By the end, I wasn't arranging shells anymore. I was building an agent workspace out of `tmux`, badly.

## Through `OpenRig` into the `Herdr` TUI

I first ran `herdr` on October 21, and not because I was shopping for a multiplexer. At the time I was wiring up [`OpenRig`](https://github.com/mvschwarz/openrig "mention | Rig project I was building at the time; it needed somewhere to put agent terminals") - pods, role-based agents, a daemon keeping them alive - whose `tmux` runtime pushed prompts through a shell shim. Six minutes after my first `herdr` command, that same rig was running with `--provider herdr` instead. Until that day, I had filed `Herdr` away as just another `tmux` clone and never opened it.

<YouTube id="AL-PQuB2wy0" />

Inside the TUI, the whole picture shifted. Agents had names and state badges, workers could prompt siblings directly, and scripts could block on a teammate instead of a guessed timer. I put a team in one workspace and wrote three small wrappers - `team say`, `team wait`, and `team read` - that made coordination a one-liner.

What didn't hold was the layer on top. I built an extension bridge so an [`omp`](https://github.com/can1357/oh-my-pi "mention | The coding agent I run; opinionated fork of Pi") seat could render `omp`'s own TUI inside the rig, opened it as [a draft PR](https://github.com/mvschwarz/openrig/pull/1747 "further-reading | The draft PR for that bridge; parked, not merged"), and then parked it when a *proposed* cleaner harness contract made my patch obsolete. That was the end of my `OpenRig` detour. `OpenRig` got me to open `Herdr`, even if it wasn't what kept me there.

Still, the TUI didn't solve the window problem. The team lived inside that terminal session, which meant keeping it open to see what was happening - and none of those TUI agents showed up in the desktop app that came next. I finally had a place to put the herd, but I still had to carry the box around.

## Core under the app

Ironically, what pulled me over to the headless architecture was the desktop client. A post about the new app showed vertical tabs - the exact reason I had been running `cmux` - alongside live agent status badges next to terminal names. Digging into how it worked led me to `herdr-core`, which turned out to be the real foundation.

Core runs terminals without drawing a single pixel. I start a terminal, address it by name through the `hc` CLI, send it work, wait, and read the output while it runs completely off-screen.

More importantly, the status badges aren't guesswork. Programs report their own state over [`OSC 7501`](https://github.com/herdrdev/herdr-core/blob/master/schema/README.md "reference | The Program Status Protocol schema"), the Program Status Protocol:

```bash
printf '\e]7501;state=blocked:app=my-agent\e\\'
```

Core does not scrape terminal frames or maintain a hardcoded list of agent brands - it simply trusts what the process reports. If a program stays silent, it gets no badge, and `hc wait` falls back to waiting for exit. That boundary matters. A status badge is only useful when the program declares its state, not when a UI tries to guess from screen text. The vocabulary is small - `working`, `blocked`, `done`, `idle`, `error` - and I have not needed a sixth. I don't need a progress bar; I just need to know who is waiting on me, who is finished, and who is still running.

`omp` reports on its own. `Hermes` did not, so I wrote a small plugin, and it has worked since the first try. By the time I decided to publish it in case someone else was stuck on the same gap, [someone had shipped a plugin covering part of it two days earlier](https://github.com/jspoynor/herdr-hermes-status "mention | Public Hermes status plugin, narrower in scope: Python CLI only, no desktop or gateway"). That is the entire cost of being late: fifteen minutes of work instead of an afternoon of integration, because the protocol is one escape sequence.

With Core, the team finally has shape: a space for the project, a tab for the task, panes for agents, and sub-agents nested directly under their lead. That nesting isn't cosmetic - I can see which worker belongs to which lead without keeping a mental map. My first real Core run - an `omp` pane replying `READY`, `hc wait` returning `done` on turn one, and tearing the pane down - was boring in the exact way good plumbing should be.

## The desktop is where I work

`herdr-app` turns those background terminals into a workspace I actually want to sit in: projects down the sidebar, tasks in tabs, agents in panes, and sub-agents tucked one click beneath the lead that spawned them.

The feature I didn't expect to rely on so heavily is peeking. `terminal.peek` lets me glance into a worker without reshuffling my layout; `terminal.show` promotes it to a real pane once it earns the screen space. For years I assumed visibility meant giving every worker a permanent tile. Now the terminals keep running in the background while I decide which single one deserves my attention right now.

There are rough edges worth noting:

- `layout.apply` is destructive - it can kill and recreate shells - so I don't treat it as a harmless resize command.
- There is no `pane.rename` or documented one-shot tiling verb, which is why my own `hgrid.py` script is still around.
- There is no documented way to move a live terminal directly between tabs.
- Core does not restore running agent state after a server reboot - a kept terminal comes back as a fresh shell in its old directory, so persistence is not session resurrection.
- [App mods](https://herdr.dev/docs/mods "reference | Mods are scripts that add features to the desktop app") are trusted JavaScript with full network and command access; I wouldn't install one blindly from a random thread.

I would rather keep a couple of small wrapper scripts for those gaps than pretend they aren't there.

## The same herd on my phone

Away from the desk, the mobile client is where this setup goes from "nice workspace" to the part I would miss. The [`Herdr` phone app](https://herdr.dev/mobile/ "reference | Official mobile client; connects to herdr-core over SSH, no account or relay") connects straight to `herdr-core` over SSH - no `Herdr` account, no vendor relay - and groups your agents into an Inbox sorted by needs you, done, working, and idle. Tapping an agent opens its live terminal so I can read the output and reply without disturbing my desktop panes.

April is when I last tried to solve this, and I solved it on paper: [`Blink`](https://blink.sh "reference | iOS terminal built on Mosh, the strongest open-source option I tried"), [`Moshi`](https://getmoshi.app "reference | iOS terminal whose workflow fitted best"), [`Mosh`](https://mosh.org "reference | Mobile shell that survives roaming and sleep"), [`Rootshell`](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rootshell-local-terminal-ssh/id6755794662 "mention | Local terminal and SSH client for iOS"), [`iSH Shell`](https://ish.app "mention | Linux shell emulator for iOS; iOS suspends it in the background"), `tmux`, the whole phone-to-host chain, with a `Moshi` + `Mosh` + `tmux` stack that looked like it would work. I prepared the host, then never set the push token, so the notifications that were the entire point never fired once. What I wanted was to see and act on the same work from the sofa, not squint at a session that might have quietly dropped while I was away from it.

Now I open the phone and the herd is there. The app is six days old as I write this, so I will not claim the setup is frictionless for everyone. It needs `herdr-core` running, one install command on the box, and SSH reachability, which for me means [`Tailscale`](https://tailscale.com "reference | WireGuard mesh I use to reach this box from anywhere"), which is how I reach this box from anywhere anyway. In the time I have used it I have not hit a real bug. It does not offer a separate approval card and it will not let me edit the desktop layout, and when an agent needs me I open its terminal and answer there, which is what I was going to do anyway.

## New glass over familiar steel

The three things `Herdr` stands on were all older than `Herdr`, which made trusting it easy.

The terminal engine is [`Ghostty`](https://ghostty.org "reference | Terminal emulator whose VT library all three Herdr products are built on")'s: `herdr-app`, the TUI, and `herdr-core` all build against vendored [`libghostty-vt`](https://github.com/ghostty-org/ghostty "reference | Ghostty's terminal library, vendored by all three Herdr products") bindings. Having used `Ghostty` directly, then inside `cmux`, and now inside `Herdr`, it's the same engine in its third wrapper.

The agents stay ordinary CLI programs. Core manages a pseudo-terminal per session via `spawn_pty`; `hc run` launches any command, `hc send` types into it, and `hc read` pulls the screen and scrollback. Both run here as ordinary programs; only `Hermes` needed a small plugin to report its state. My team orchestration script is a few hundred lines calling a plain CLI, which means I can read and fix it at midnight instead of debugging someone's plugin SDK.

Even the desktop UI stands on proven ground: `herdr-app` vendors [`GPUI`](https://github.com/zed-industries/zed/tree/main/crates/gpui "reference | Zed's GPU-accelerated UI framework, vendored by herdr-app"), [`Zed`](https://zed.dev "reference | The editor GPUI comes from")'s UI framework. I don't need to know the internals to appreciate the result - a fast sidebar, tabs, and panes that mirror the TUI layout I already knew how to drive on day one.

I expected a trade-off and did not find one that costs me anything I use. Agents still run in real terminals, non-reporting harnesses take fifteen minutes to wire up, and a reboot hands back a shell in the last working directory, which is what `tmux` gave me too. Nothing I relied on got worse.

## A place, not a vendor bet

What keeps me invested beyond any single client is the open protocol underneath. Because `OSC 7501` is an open escape sequence, any program running in a core terminal that emits its state lands in the exact same Inbox regardless of who built it - `omp` natively, `Hermes` via a tiny plugin, or a bash script of my own. `Herdr` doesn't need a partnership with an AI vendor just to show whether a process is thinking or blocked.

That is the difference between a place where agents live and a launcher for one company's product. `tmux` still handles plain panes and persistence just fine. The `Herdr` TUI proved agents could be visible and coordinate, even though its agents never turned up in the app. Moving to `herdr-core` decoupled the agents from the glass - letting me work from a clean desktop layout at my desk, or clear a blocked agent from the sofa, without keeping a wall of terminals open all day.

I stopped keeping every agent on screen. Now I check the herd when I need it.
